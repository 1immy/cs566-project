Introduction

Single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) cameras capture high-speed bursts of binary frames, where each pixel records only whether a photon arrived during a given exposure. A single frame carries almost no usable signal; a clean image only emerges from aligning and merging an entire burst, a process known as quanta burst photography. We address single-photon image reconstruction under motion: specifically, whether history-rejection heuristics from real-time temporal anti-aliasing (TAA), namely neighborhood clamping and motion-adaptive blending, improve classical align-and-merge reconstruction, especially under fast motion and disocclusion.

The standard SPAD reconstruction pipeline (estimate motion, align, merge) is structurally identical to temporal anti-aliasing in real-time rendering, yet this connection does not appear to have been explored in prior work. Bringing history-rejection techniques developed for real-time graphics into photon-level reconstruction offers a genuinely cross-domain angle with a clear, testable hypothesis. SPAD arrays are also an emerging sensor technology (megapixel-scale arrays now exist), which makes the underlying reconstruction problem increasingly practical to study.

The project builds directly on Prof. Gupta's own quanta burst photography research and the Single Photon Challenge benchmark his lab co-organized, giving the team a public dataset with ground truth rather than requiring custom data collection.

Related Work

Quanta burst photography (Gupta et al.) establishes the align-then-accumulate pipeline for SPAD bursts that serves as our baseline.

Temporal anti-aliasing. A survey of TAA techniques (Yang et al., NVIDIA, 2020) catalogs the history-rejection methods we adapt to this domain: neighborhood clamping and motion-adaptive blend weights.

Single Photon Challenge. This benchmark provides the dataset and existing leaderboard submissions used for comparison in this work.

Approach

Our approach proceeds in four stages:

  1. Implement a baseline: naive frame averaging, followed by hierarchical block alignment and merging (a simplified quanta burst photography pipeline).
  2. Implement a TAA-augmented pipeline using the same alignment and merge backbone, adding neighborhood-clamping history rejection, motion-adaptive blend weights, and a per-pixel confidence map.
  3. Evaluate both pipelines on held-out Single Photon Challenge training scenes and on synthetic motion sweeps generated with the visionsim simulator.
  4. Analyze where and why each method breaks down under fast motion and disocclusion.

Figure 1 illustrates the per-frame mechanism of the TAA-augmented pipeline and the point at which the naive-averaging baseline diverges from it. This approach draws on course material including image alignment and transformations, RANSAC, optical flow, tracking, and the quanta computational imaging lecture.

Data

The Single Photon Challenge dataset provides 50 simulated training scenes and 5 test scenes (ground truth withheld), each burst (a “photon cube”) consisting of 1,024 binary frames. The full set is approximately 425 GB; we will work with a 10-scene shared subset (about 5 GB; the average scene archive is about 2.7 GB, not the 8.5 GB stated in the proposal), supplemented with our own synthetic motion sweeps generated via the MIT-licensed visionsim simulator.

Evaluation Plan

We will report PSNR and SSIM on held-out training scenes, and conduct a failure analysis against motion speed using visionsim sweeps to characterize when temporal reconstruction breaks down. The contribution is framed as an interpretable classical method with a rigorous failure analysis, rather than a state-of-the-art claim against neural approaches.

Project Updates

Mid-Term Report (Oct 29, 2026)

Both pipeline stages now run on real Single Photon Challenge data, and an evaluation harness reports PSNR and SSIM. Two early findings shape the rest of the project: the scene moves inside a single burst, and the ground truth is at the pose of the last frames. The mid-term table is at the end of this section; the final results are in the dated Final Results section below.

Download the mid-term report (PDF)

Progress

Data and infrastructure. A loader reads the 1,024-frame bit-packed bursts with frame and crop selection, so a 2 GB cube never has to be unpacked in full. A 1 MB sample is committed to the repository and a 10-scene slice (about 5 GB) is shared with the team. The full set stays on a private NAS for the final runs.

Alignment. Coarse-to-fine 8×8 block matching on a three-level pyramid, applied to group means (64 frames per group). Every method shares this aligner, so differences between methods come from the merge stage.

History validation (TAA). The warped history is clamped to the 3×3 neighborhood range of the incoming group and merged as a weighted mean, discounted by a confidence that falls with neighborhood disagreement and with displacement. Switching both components off recovers the aligned baseline.

Evaluation. evaluate(reconstruction, ground_truth) returns mean per-channel PSNR and SSIM. A harness runs naive, aligned and TAA pipelines (and plug-in pipelines) on a list of bursts and writes a results table and comparison figures. A report-table mode reproduces the five variants of the results table with the same scene, crop, frames and references. All 90 tests pass.

Findings

The scene moves inside a single burst. Phase correlation between 256-frame windows shows a mostly horizontal drift of about 9, 20 and 2 px across the three bursts we measured, so alignment has to work on groups of frames rather than single binary frames.

The ground truth is at the pose of the last frames. Aligning to the first group is 2.2 to 2.9 dB worse than naive averaging, while aligning to the last group is 2.5 to 4.4 dB better. Under the per-image gain of the first table, our first TAA configuration is 1.0 to 3.8 dB worse than plain alignment, because its displacement-based confidence discards correct frames once the motion has been compensated. Under the fixed-gain harness, TAA is within 0.2 dB of last-group alignment in PSNR but about 0.04 lower in SSIM.

Difficulties

  • Reference frame. A fixed first-group reference, the natural default, makes alignment look harmful. We found this only after comparing against the ground truth at several time offsets.
  • Intensity mismatch. The ground truth is much brighter than the photon average. We currently use two rules, a per-image gain for the first table and a fixed gain of 2.7 in the harness (the kitchen1 table under Final Results), and one rule has to be chosen and applied everywhere.
  • Noise versus motion. Single binary frames cannot be aligned, and photon noise in flat regions produces apparent motion, which makes displacement an unreliable proxy for alignment error.
  • TAA tuning. Once alignment has compensated for the motion, the displacement term in our confidence discards correct frames and raises noise.

Changes to the proposal

  • Alignment unit. Alignment runs within a burst, on groups of frames, with the last group as reference.
  • Motion sweeps. Real bursts already span roughly 2 to 20 px of motion, so the visionsim sweeps become optional and the motion study uses real bursts binned by measured speed.
  • Data size. The average archive is about 2.7 GB (133 GB compressed in total), not 8.5 GB as proposed.
  • Hypothesis. The initial TAA result is negative: when alignment is accurate, displacement-based rejection hurts. We now plan a confidence based on alignment residual and will report the failure analysis either way.

Next steps

The harness already reproduces the last-group variants on full bursts. Next we unify the intensity rule, isolate whether the gain rule or the improved aligner explains the smaller TAA gap, and rerun on a motion-binned scene set. Replace the displacement term with a residual-based confidence, run ablations (clamp, blend, confidence) and a failure analysis against motion speed. Experiments freeze on Nov 30.

Final Results (Nov 23, 2026)

These are the final results. They replace the preliminary single-scene numbers of the mid-term checkpoint above, which used a different intensity rule.

Every number below uses one protocol: 1,024-frame bursts, groups of 64, the last group as reference, one fixed gain of 2.7 for every method, and the same aligner and photon cube for naive averaging, aligned averaging and TAA. PSNR and SSIM are computed on the reconstructed frame, the full frame unless a crop is stated. The first experiment is the broad one: 31 real bursts from 29 training scenes, grouped by how far the scene moves during the burst.

Thirty were chosen as the bursts closest to each bin’s median (distinct scenes first); the thirty-first, attic 0, was run earlier and kept.   • TAA and alignment have the same PSNR on average (+0.08 dB over all bursts, interval from −0.18 to +0.34). TAA is ahead on 14 bursts and behind on 17 (exact sign test, p = 0.72), so there is no consistent winner.
  • TAA has lower SSIM than alignment on every one of the 31 bursts (−0.073 on average, interval −0.088 to −0.058; sign test p < 0.001), and in every bin. The deficit is −0.056 for slow, −0.070 for medium and −0.092 for fast bursts; the slow and fast intervals overlap, so we do not claim it grows with speed.
  • Alignment beats naive averaging by 0.78 dB PSNR and 0.092 SSIM on average. The PSNR gain rises from 0.48 to 0.80 to 1.05 dB from slow to fast bins, while the SSIM gain falls from 0.107 to 0.065.

Caveat on the bins. 80 of the 100 measured bursts, and 26 of the 31 reconstructed ones, have a weak phase-correlation peak (below 0.05), and every burst with a good peak has a measured drift under about 19 px. Large motion smears the window averages and lowers the peak, so the drift of the medium and fast bursts may be real but coarsely measured, or partly noise; we cannot separate the two. Read “fast” as “large measured drift”, not as a known speed.

Both draw binary frames from the same Bernoulli model and are scored with the same protocol. The “measured” drift is about three quarters of the nominal value, because it spans only the 768 frames between window centres.

Discussion (Nov 23, 2026)

What the results show

  • The reference frame matters more than anything TAA adds. The ground truth is the pose at the end of the burst. Aligning the groups to the last group beats naive averaging by 1.6 dB on kitchen1, and aligning to the first group is 1.5 dB worse than not aligning at all.
  • Beyond alignment, TAA history rejection gives no consistent PSNR gain and costs SSIM. Over 31 real bursts the mean PSNR difference is +0.08 dB with an interval that spans zero, and TAA has lower SSIM than alignment on every burst.
  • TAA helps on some bursts and hurts on others. It is 1 to 2 dB ahead on three fast bursts where alignment leaves blocky artifacts (for example bathroom3 1), and 1 to 2 dB behind on very noisy ones (ultramodern 0, bathroom5 1). We do not have a rule that predicts which.
  • We did not find a speed at which TAA clearly fails or clearly wins on real data. The SSIM deficit is present in every bin, and the PSNR difference stays inside its interval in every bin.

Why, and what we did not test

The mid-term result was that a displacement-based confidence discards correct frames once alignment has compensated the motion, so history rejection removes information it should keep; the component controls on kitchen1 are consistent with that (clamping alone matches alignment, the full configuration does not). We think a second reason is that TAA retains more grain than plain averaging of aligned groups, which SSIM penalises more than PSNR does, and it would explain why TAA can look cleaner to the eye and still score lower in SSIM. We did not test this. A confidence based on the alignment residual, planned at mid-term, was not implemented or evaluated in this project.

Limits

  • Measured speed is unreliable for medium and fast bursts. 26 of the 31 reconstructed bursts have a weak motion estimate, so the bins show large measured drift, not a known speed. The controlled sweeps know the speed but are one scene and one seed each, and the simulated and the rendered sweep disagree about TAA at 40 px.
  • Small samples. Ten or eleven bursts per bin, two of 29 scenes contributing two bursts, and bootstrap intervals that are close to the range of the observed differences.
  • One intensity rule. A fixed gain of 2.7 for every scene may fit some scenes poorly, which compresses all differences; PSNR on the real bursts is 11 to 22 dB.
  • Simple motion model. The sweeps model only photon noise and a global translation; real scenes also contain disocclusion, parallax and local motion.
  • Training scenes only. The five test scenes have no public ground truth, so every number comes from training scenes.
